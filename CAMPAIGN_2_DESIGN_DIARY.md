# Adaptive English · Campaign 2 Design Diary

Purpose: capture lessons learned during Campaign 1 that should influence Campaign 2, without altering the current campaign.

## Core design principle

Hooks are scaffolding, not grammar.

Campaign 2 should deliberately test whether a useful shortcut has become an overgeneralised rule.

Preferred learning cycle:

HOOK → AUTOMATION → ANTI-HOOK / CONTRAST → SPACED RETENTION

A hook is considered robust only when the learner can:
1. use it quickly in its valid domain;
2. reject it outside that domain;
3. survive deliberately misleading surface cues;
4. retrieve the real rule after a spaced interval.

---

## Entry 001 · Conditional length is not grammar

### Observed Campaign 1 shortcut
Long sentence → probably third conditional.
Short sentence → probably second conditional.

### Why it helped
Some Campaign 1 templates correlated phrase length with conditional type, so length became a fast perceptual cue.

### Risk
The learner may answer by sentence shape rather than time reference.

### Campaign 2 anti-hook
Deliberately include:
- very short third conditionals;
- long second conditionals;
- short mixed conditionals;
- items where length points toward the wrong superficial category.

Examples:
- If I'd known, I'd have gone. → third conditional, short.
- If I lived in a city where transport was cheaper and more reliable, I'd probably stop driving every day. → second conditional, long.

### Real rule to preserve
Second conditional → unreal/hypothetical present or future.
Third conditional → unreal past / counterfactual past.
Mixed conditional → condition and result belong to different time frames.

## Entry 002 · ALLOW hook needs an anti-hook

### Useful Campaign 1 hook
ALLOW + someone → TO + verb.

Examples:
- They allowed me to leave.
- The app allows users to upload files.

### Risk
Overgeneralisation into: ALLOW always takes TO.

Incorrect generalisation:
- They allow to smoke. ✗

### Campaign 2 anti-hook
Contrast:
- allow + object + to-infinitive
- allow + -ing when there is no object
- be allowed + to-infinitive

Examples:
- They allow visitors to smoke outside.
- They don't allow smoking here.
- Visitors are allowed to smoke outside.

### Retrieval rule
ALLOW + SOMEONE → TO
ALLOW + ACTIVITY → -ING
BE ALLOWED → TO

## Entry 003 · MAKE hook needs later boundary testing

### Useful Campaign 1 hook
MAKE/MADE → NO TO · MAKE ME FEEL

Active:
- The error made him check the settings.

### Campaign 2 anti-hook
Test the passive contrast:
- He was made to check the settings.

### Real rule
Active make + object → infinitivo sin to.
Passive be made → to-infinitive.

Do not introduce this contrast in Campaign 1 if it would contaminate the current acquisition target. Save it for later transfer testing.

## Entry 004 · DESPITE / ALTHOUGH should progress from cue to transfer

### Current fast cue
DESPITE → COSA / -ING
ALTHOUGH → FRASE (subject + finite verb)

Examples:
- despite the heavy traffic
- although the traffic was heavy

### Campaign 2 anti-hook
Avoid making all DESPITE items visibly short and all ALTHOUGH items visibly long.
Use semantically equivalent pairs with similar length.

Also test:
- despite being tired
- although she was tired

Goal: grammatical structure, not visual sentence length, should drive the answer.

## Entry 005 · IF / UNLESS must measure polarity, not author intention

### Campaign 1 lesson
Ambiguous isolated clauses made the learner guess the intended scenario.

Campaign 1 v3.16 corrected the bank so conditions are clearer.

### Campaign 2 design rule
Every IF / UNLESS item should have enough context to make the intended polarity clear.

Timed retrieval:
- IF → positive condition
- UNLESS → a menos que

Slow verification:
- unless P ≈ if not P

### Anti-hook
Do not let negative main clauses mechanically predict UNLESS.
Create clear examples where:
- negative main clause + IF is correct;
- positive main clause + UNLESS is possible when semantically appropriate.

The semantic relation must win over sentence polarity.

## Entry 006 · Mixed conditionals: surface formula is not enough

### Campaign 1 lesson
A single mixed-conditional formula was insufficient because both temporal directions exist.

### Real retrieval operation
MIXED → identify the TWO time frames first.

Direction A:
past cause → present result
If + had + participle → would + infinitivo sin to now

Direction B:
present unreal condition → past result
If + past simple → would have + participle

### Campaign 2 anti-hook
Reduce dependence on obvious words such as now, yesterday and last year once the structure becomes strong.
Use semantic time reference as well as explicit time adverbs.

---

## Entry 007 · Past modal deduction: distinguish function before form

### Emerging shortcut
Evidence → MUST HAVE
Reproach/regret → SHOULD HAVE
Past impossibility → CAN'T HAVE

### Campaign 2 anti-hook
Use the same participle and similar sentence shape across all three, so the learner must read the evidence rather than recognise a lexical template.

Goal:
meaning first, modal second.

## Campaign 2 generator rules

When a Campaign 1 hook reaches stable mastery, Campaign 2 should introduce adversarial-but-fair items that:
- break accidental correlations with sentence length;
- break accidental correlations with positive/negative wording;
- vary lexical content while preserving the same grammar;
- include valid neighbouring constructions that expose overgeneralisation;
- remain semantically unambiguous;
- never depend on guessing the author's intended story;
- preserve the fixed timed-game principle while making the grammatical signal clean.

## Evidence policy

Do not promote a learner observation into a Campaign 2 rule after one anecdote.

Record:
- the observed shortcut;
- why it may have formed;
- whether it is grammatical or merely statistical;
- the anti-hook needed;
- examples that falsify the shortcut;
- the underlying rule that must survive.
